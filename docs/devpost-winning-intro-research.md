# Devpost-winning intro patterns

Research date: 2026-08-27

## Question

How do winning Devpost pages establish their argument in the first visible sentence, tagline, or opening paragraph?

## Limits

- This is a qualitative sample of ten public projects carrying an explicit Devpost winner label, across different hackathons and years.
- A winner page shows what was present in a winning submission; it does **not** prove that its wording caused the win.
- I found no primary source supporting the claim that Devpost judges spend literally three seconds on each submission. Devpost does say judges may handle many submissions, that videos provide context, and that presentation and storytelling help keep judges engaged.

## Patterns actually present

1. **The tagline carries real work.** Stronger pages do not spend it on category language such as “AI-powered platform.” They name an outcome or action: launch, detect and respond, convert, connect, or automate.
2. **The opening reaches the mechanism quickly.** The problem is not only an emotion. It names what fails: spreadsheets during a crisis, a missing classroom feedback loop, a tedious listing process, or an inaccessible financial threshold.
3. **Specificity substitutes for hype.** Concrete inputs, outputs, time, cost, or operating context make the claim inspectable: `$50`, `18 months`, `listing to pickup`, `video to listing`, `detect and launch`.
4. **The product follows the friction immediately.** Even story-led openings usually disclose the product or its work in the next sentence or section.
5. **Several valid shapes win.** Some start with a scene, others with a declarative product result, a quantified problem, or a personal experience. A dramatic question is not a common requirement.
6. **The page and demo complement each other.** The sampled pages place a short tagline and media before the detailed story. The text establishes relevance; the demo carries execution proof.

## Official Devpost guidance

Devpost's [interviews with five judges](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips) report that judges first check requirements, then use the video/demo for context and evaluate against the rubric. The judges also say creativity, enthusiasm, visual appeal, performance, presentation, and storytelling affect whether a project holds attention. They warn against ambiguity, thin implementation, rehashed ideas, and over-indexing on only one criterion.

Devpost's [submission and judging guide](https://info.devpost.com/blog/understanding-hackathon-submission-and-judging-criteria) says a demo should clearly and concisely explain the project, features, and benefits; a genuine problem and potential impact should be explicit; and the product should be explainable in one or two sentences.

Devpost's [judging help page](https://help.devpost.com/article/103-how-to-judge-an-online-hackathon) confirms that judges score each submission against the hackathon's listed criteria. This supports writing for the rubric, not inventing a universal “three-second” judging rule.

## Inference for Shoots

The useful pattern is not “begin with a clever question.” It is:

> **recognizable photographer situation -> missing feedback mechanism -> autonomous work -> checkable result**

Shoots should therefore avoid opening with either:

- an abstract metaphor such as “a camera roll cannot show learning”; or
- the broad question “Am I improving?” without first grounding what makes it unanswerable.

A better first-screen argument is structurally:

> Hobbyist photographers take hundreds of Shots but receive feedback one image at a time. A good Shot still cannot tell them whether a decision was deliberate, repeatable, or lucky.
>
> Shoots observes every approved Camera Shot, closes each natural Shoot into a learning record, and tracks which photographic decisions recur, which can be reproduced, and what changes over time—with every claim linked to its supporting Shots.

This is a structural recommendation, not final copy. It earns the question “Am I improving, or just getting lucky?” by explaining the mechanism first, and it surfaces Taskmaster work before a judge reaches the architecture section.

## Recommended opening order

1. **One plain sentence:** who is stuck, doing what, and why ordinary evidence cannot answer them.
2. **One work sentence:** what Shoots autonomously processes and the artifact it leaves behind.
3. **One proof sentence:** how the judge or photographer can inspect the claim.
4. Then expand into the lived story, existing alternatives, agent loop, and architecture.

Do not imitate the weakest winner pages merely because they won. The sample establishes allowable patterns, not a formula. For this hackathon, the opening should also mirror its unusually explicit rubric: real friction, autonomous execution, and proof of action.
